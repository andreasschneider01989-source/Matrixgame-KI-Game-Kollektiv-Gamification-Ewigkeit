# Emotion Regulation and Goal Alignment — Matrixgame framework

**Date:** 2026-11-25  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** EVIDENCE-INFORMED DESIGN MODEL  
**Implementation:** documentation and one optional quest template; no neural monitoring, personal mood database, diagnostic system, or adaptive therapy engine is implemented.

## 1. Purpose

This module turns the wish for more constructive, purposeful and emotionally sustainable thinking into a practical model. Its goal is not to suppress negative emotions or manufacture constant positivity. The goal is to notice emotional signals, understand their possible information, choose a proportionate response, and return attention to a personally meaningful next step.

An emotion is not a software defect, an instruction, or proof that an interpretation is correct. It is an experience shaped by bodily state, appraisal, memory, context, needs, expectations and social conditions. The same label can mean different things in different situations.

**Core rule:** An emotion may deserve attention without being allowed to dictate the next action.

## 2. WHO health perspective

The WHO Constitution defines health through physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease. WHO further describes mental health as more than the absence of mental disorder: it includes the ability to cope with normal stresses, realize abilities, work productively and contribute to community life.

This definition is a broad public-health ideal, not a pass/fail score that one person must maintain every day. Human lives include grief, fear, anger, tiredness, uncertainty and periods of reduced capacity. A realistic model supports recovery, relationships, agency and functioning rather than demanding permanent positive mood.

Sources:
- WHO Constitution: https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution/
- WHO Mental Health: https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/themes/theme-details/GHO/mental-health
- WHO Doing What Matters in Times of Stress: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240003927

## 3. What current research suggests

### 3.1 Reappraisal and suppression are not interchangeable

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Nature Human Behaviour* synthesized 249 articles, 150,474 participants and 37 countries/regions. It examined cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression. The relationships with mental-health outcomes varied across cultural and demographic contexts. Reappraisal was generally more adaptive in some contexts; suppression was more maladaptive in some, but the findings do not justify a universal rule for every person or situation.

Practical distinction:
- **Reappraisal:** check whether another interpretation is plausible, without denying facts or real harm.
- **Suppression:** attempt to hide or push away the felt emotion. It may be temporarily useful in some social or safety contexts, but relying on it as the only strategy can be costly.

Source:
- Chen et al. (2025), *Emotion regulation and mental health across cultures: a systematic review and meta-analysis*: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02168-8

### 3.2 Flexibility matters

A 2026 meta-analytic review examined links between cognitive control and emotion-regulation strategies across 215 studies. Stronger cognitive control showed small associations with more effective reappraisal and less rumination and worry; the authors emphasize that effects were modest and measurement remains difficult.

A separate 2026 systematic review of expressive flexibility reported associations with well-being and resilience, but also substantial heterogeneity and uncertainty. These findings support flexible strategy choice, not a guaranteed technique or a single optimal emotional style.

Sources:
- Schulze et al. (2026), *Rethinking the link between cognitive control and emotion regulation*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106708
- *Emotional Expressive Flexibility and Mental Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis* (2026): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13702306/

### 3.3 Body and mind are connected

WHO physical-activity guidance notes that physical activity benefits physical and mental health, can reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety, and can support thinking, learning and well-being. Its adult guideline is 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent amount of vigorous activity; any amount is better than none.

This is population guidance, not a personal prescription. Ability, health, accessibility and medical advice can change what is appropriate.

Source:
- WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour: https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789240014886

### 3.4 Practical stress skills

WHO's *Doing What Matters in Times of Stress* teaches grounding, making room for difficult feelings, stepping back from unhelpful thoughts, acting on values, and kindness toward oneself and others. These can be practised in small moments. They are self-help skills, not a guarantee of symptom removal and not a replacement for professional care when needed.

## 4. Matrixgame regulation loop: SIGNAL → CLARIFY → CHOOSE → ACT → REVIEW

1. **SIGNAL — notice:** What am I experiencing? Where do I notice it in my body? Is the intensity low, moderate or high? A rough self-rating is optional, not an objective biological measurement.
2. **CLARIFY — separate:** What happened? What am I interpreting? What am I predicting? What remains unknown? Do not confuse a thought, fear or assumption with an established fact.
3. **UNDERSTAND — identify possible function:** Is the signal pointing to danger, loss, injustice, a blocked goal, fatigue, uncertainty, unmet need, value conflict or a request for connection? These are prompts, not fixed emotion-to-meaning rules.
4. **CHOOSE — select a fitting strategy:** change the situation if possible; redirect attention; test a more balanced interpretation; accept what cannot be changed right now; communicate a boundary; ask for help; or rest.
5. **ACT — choose the smallest useful step:** one action that is safe, proportionate and aligned with the person's chosen values.
6. **REVIEW — learn and recover:** Did the action help? Is more information needed? Should the next step be smaller, different, social, or postponed? Rest and pausing are valid outcomes.

### Strategy selection

| Situation | Possible response | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| A real and immediate threat | Prioritize safety, distance and appropriate help | Do not reframe away genuine danger |
| Uncertain interpretation | Check facts, ask a question, consider alternatives | Positive reinterpretation is not proof |
| A solvable obstacle | Break it into one small problem-solving step | Do not turn every feeling into a productivity task |
| Loss or disappointment | Allow emotion, seek support, take time | Do not demand instant positivity |
| Boundary violation or injustice | Name the boundary, document facts, seek support | Anger can signal something important, but does not dictate retaliation |
| Fatigue or overload | Reduce demands, recover, sleep or pause | Do not label reduced capacity as personal failure |
| Rumination without new information | Ground attention, write one concern and one next step, then disengage | If persistent or impairing, seek additional support |

No emotion should be automatically classified as "bad". No emotion should automatically be obeyed. Context, intensity, duration and consequences matter.

## 5. Goal alignment: personal aims and shared human aims

A constructive goal system distinguishes:

- **Personal values:** what the person freely considers meaningful.
- **Chosen goals:** concrete outcomes compatible with those values.
- **Shared public-good aims:** safety, dignity, health, knowledge, cooperation, sustainability and fair opportunity.
- **Constraints:** available energy, money, time, health, consent, rights and real-world feasibility.

Look for overlap, but do not assume every collective objective overrides personal needs. A good plan respects agency and can be renegotiated.

The project's responsible-gamification standard already emphasizes autonomy, competence, relatedness, meaning, flow and recovery. This module reuses those principles: choice, achievable steps, honest feedback, optional collaboration and no punitive streaks or public emotional rankings.

A simple implementation intention can help:
- "If I notice myself looping over an uncertain thought, then I will write down the observable fact, the interpretation and one question I could verify."
- "If I feel overloaded, then I will choose a smaller task or a pause rather than intensifying pressure."

These are optional plans, not mandatory scripts.

## 6. Link to the WHO physical–mental–social dimensions

| Dimension | Examples of chosen goals | Possible observable signals |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | movement, regular meals, sleep routine, recovery, appropriate care | chosen activity, energy availability, sleep regularity, functional comfort |
| Mental | emotional flexibility, learning, purpose, creativity, concentration | ability to return to a chosen task, perceived clarity, manageable stress |
| Social | connection, communication, trust, consent, contribution, boundaries | meaningful contact, mutual support, respectful communication |

Do not infer an individual's health from a single metric. These dimensions influence one another and are affected by social and environmental conditions.

## 7. Privacy and safety

- No brain, neural, physiological or emotional state is directly observable through this chat.
- Do not claim to rewire, control or directly optimize a person's neurons.
- Personal reflections remain private by default; do not publish them to GitHub.
- No mood surveillance, diagnosis, personal ranking, productivity score or compulsory daily check-in.
- The participant can skip, pause, adapt or decline every exercise.
- AI may help organize thoughts and propose options; the person retains decision authority.
- Serious, persistent or rapidly worsening distress, major loss of everyday functioning, or safety concerns warrant human support from an appropriate professional or trusted person. Immediate danger requires local emergency support.
- Never treat positive mood as the only success criterion. A useful boundary, an honest conversation, grieving, asking for help or choosing rest can all be healthy actions.

## 8. Evaluation without pressure

Evaluate the tool, not the worth of the person.

Possible opt-in review questions:
- Did the loop help distinguish facts from interpretations?
- Did it make a useful choice easier?
- Did pressure, rumination or confusion decrease, stay the same or increase?
- Was the chosen action consistent with personal values?
- Did the process respect energy, time and privacy?

Do not claim causality from one short interaction or one mood rating. For any future study, document context, sample size, measurement method, uncertainty, missing data and possible adverse effects.

## 9. Small Matrixgame implementation plan

1. Use the existing Quest schema; do not create a second personal-data format.
2. Add one optional, low-effort quest template that lets a participant choose among grounding, clarification, problem-solving, connection and recovery.
3. Keep personal quest runs local/private and separate from public definitions.
4. Link the quest to the Human Flourishing framework's physical, mental and social dimensions.
5. Test only the public template and validation rules; never upload an individual's emotional answers.
6. Later, if the user explicitly wants it, build a local-only reflection interface with no automatic tracking and no diagnostic outputs.

## 10. Design principle

**Feelings are signals, not commands. Values are directions, not weapons. Progress is useful when it supports life rather than consuming it.**

The aim is not a person who never feels fear, sadness, anger or uncertainty. It is a person who can increasingly understand these experiences, respond flexibly, seek connection when useful, and continue toward meaningful goals without unnecessary self-punishment.
