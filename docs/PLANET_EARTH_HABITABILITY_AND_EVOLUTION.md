## 1. Mission

Improve the conditions that allow life and human societies to thrive, while protecting biodiversity, human wellbeing, and planetary systems.

**Core principle:** improve the capacity of ecosystems and communities to sustain life; do not optimize a single metric such as biomass, oxygen, energy production, or carbon removal at the expense of ecological integrity.

## 2. Scientific framing

Habitability is multidimensional:

- **Physical:** temperature, liquid water, pressure, radiation, energy balance.
- **Chemical:** elements, minerals, nutrients, pH, chemical availability.
- **Biological:** metabolism, reproduction, ecological interactions, adaptation.
- **Social and technical:** health, safety, education, energy, infrastructure, governance.

Every knowledge item and proposed intervention must be labeled:

- `FACT` — supported by reliable measurements or established evidence.
- `MODEL` — explicit assumptions and equations used for a simulation.
- `HYPOTHESIS` — a testable but not yet established explanation.
- `OPEN` — unresolved question or missing data.
- `FICTION / GAME RULE` — imaginative scenario or intentionally invented mechanic.

### Important guardrails

- 37 °C is not a universal upper temperature limit for life. Human heat risk depends on humidity, radiant heat, wind, exposure, and the body's ability to dissipate heat.
- More oxygen, more trees, more biomass, or more water storage is not automatically better everywhere. Ecosystems are place-specific; nutrient-poor sand habitats and other specialized environments can be damaged by generic greening or fertilization.
- Cyanobacteria are major contributors to Earth's oxygen history and modern primary production, but harmful blooms can threaten water quality and health. Research must distinguish beneficial ecological roles from bloom risks.
- Desert restoration is not a universal tree-planting exercise. Water budgets, native species, soils, albedo, biodiversity, land rights, and long-term maintenance must be assessed before interventions.
- The Kardashev scale is a civilization-energy-use framework, not a validated forecast or a wellbeing score.
- The Hawkins scale is not a validated scientific measurement of consciousness or wellbeing. Do not use it as a diagnostic or evidence metric.

## 3. Regional pilot: Darmstadt and South Hesse

Use the Darmstadt–Pfungstadt–Eschollbrücken / Hessian Ried area as a learning and observation region, not as a claim about any private property.

Potential landscape types to study:

| System | Questions and indicators |
|---|---|
| Urban gardens and farmland | soil cover, soil moisture, organic matter, pesticide and fertilizer inputs, pollinators |
| Forests | drought stress, regeneration, species composition, deadwood, canopy temperature |
| Meadows and sand habitats | habitat-specific species, nutrient status, disturbance, invasive species |
| Rivers, lakes and wetlands | water quality, seasonal water levels, groundwater, habitat connectivity |
| Built areas | sealed surface, shade, heat exposure, rainwater retention |

### Regional quests

1. **Forest Guardian:** read local forest condition reports; distinguish observation from intervention.
2. **Water Cycle:** map rainfall, runoff, infiltration, groundwater and seasonal water demand.
3. **Biodiversity Atlas:** record habitat types and species using approved citizen-science methods.
4. **Heat and Shade:** compare shaded and exposed surfaces without treating a single measurement as a regional climate trend.
5. **Sand Habitat Protection:** learn why some nutrient-poor open habitats should not be afforested or fertilized.

No field intervention should occur in protected areas without permission and ecological assessment.

## 4. Global ecosystem research

### 4.1 Forests and ecological infrastructure

Study forests as coupled systems of vegetation, soil, fungi, insects, water, and climate. Include decomposers, termites, ants, pollinators, and other organisms as ecological actors, but test specific ecosystem-service claims rather than assuming every species has a simple engineering role.

### 4.2 Cyanobacteria and photosynthesis

Research:
- oxygenic photosynthesis and its evolutionary history;
- carbon fixation and nutrient limitation;
- cyanobacterial diversity and ecological niches;
- harmful algal/cyanobacterial blooms, toxins, and monitoring;
- controlled photobioreactors, their energy and nutrient inputs, and safe containment.

A photobioreactor is not automatically carbon-negative or energy-positive. Include lifecycle energy, nutrient sourcing, water use, contamination risk, and biomass processing.

### 4.3 Regenerative dryland restoration

Treat dryland restoration as a site-specific systems problem. Candidate interventions to compare include:
- rainfall capture and infiltration;
- soil organic matter and erosion control;
- native vegetation and agroforestry where ecologically appropriate;
- restoration of wetlands and seasonal watercourses;
- solar-powered water systems only where sustainable water budgets support them.

Every proposed intervention needs a baseline, a water budget, biodiversity safeguards, local participation, maintenance planning, and monitoring.

## 5. Planetary risks and resilience

Track interacting risks rather than one headline metric:

- extreme heat and humidity;
- drought, flood, and groundwater depletion;
- ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss;
- wildfire and soil erosion;
- ocean and freshwater stress;
- food-system vulnerability;
- energy-system emissions and material demand;
- unequal exposure and adaptive capacity.

Prefer authoritative datasets and explicit uncertainty. Avoid treating a single tipping-point threshold or speculative scenario as a deterministic forecast.

## 6. Evolution and civilization

Use the evolution of life and intelligence as a learning narrative:

1. planetary chemistry and prebiotic environments;
2. cellular life and metabolism;
3. photosynthesis and changing atmospheric chemistry;
4. multicellularity and ecological complexity;
5. nervous systems, learning, and social behavior;
6. human language, science, and institutions;
7. computing and artificial intelligence;
8. possible future space-based and interplanetary research networks.

Long-term civilization scenarios are `FICTION / GAME SCENARIO` unless tied to measurable, testable forecasts. The Kardashev scale may be used as a descriptive energy-use framework, not as a measure of moral progress or happiness.

## 7. Collective intelligence architecture

Suggested agent roles:

- **Ecologist:** habitat and biodiversity context.
- **Climate analyst:** climate observations, projections, uncertainty.
- **Hydrologist:** water budgets and catchment dynamics.
- **Soil scientist:** soil structure, nutrients, carbon and water.
- **Biologist:** species, metabolism and ecological interactions.
- **Energy analyst:** energy, emissions, materials and lifecycle costs.
- **Data engineer:** provenance, units, quality and reproducibility.
- **Simulation engineer:** models, tests, sensitivity analysis.
- **Critical reviewer:** alternative explanations, failure modes, bias.
- **Community facilitator:** local knowledge, accessibility, consent and participation.
- **Archivist / teacher:** versioned knowledge and learning pathways.

Causal chain for each quest:

`Observation → Data quality → Interpretation → Hypothesis → Model → Test → Decision → Action → Outcome → Review`

The chain must allow `UNCERTAIN`, `ABSTAIN`, and `PROPOSE ALTERNATIVE`, not just accept/reject.

## 8. Positive incentive design

Reward verified contributions rather than spectacle or raw activity:

- reproducible measurements;
- transparent uncertainty;
- useful criticism and corrections;
- successful replication;
- ecological safeguards;
- open educational materials;
- community benefit;
- reduced resource use and lifecycle impacts.

Do not award points for unverified claims, invasive interventions, excessive data collection, or activity that creates ecological harm. XP is a game mechanic, not a scientific measure of impact.

## 11. First seven learning quests

1. **Green Home:** observe and care for one suitable plant; record conditions rather than guessing.
2. **Soil Intelligence:** learn texture, structure, moisture and pH; do not infer ecosystem health from pH alone.
3. **Life Evolution:** research one organism and its ecological relationships.
4. **Water Cycle:** draw a local water pathway from rainfall to soil, groundwater, streams and evapotranspiration.
5. **Forest Guardian:** summarize one current regional forest-health source.
6. **Cyanobacteria Research:** explain photosynthesis and distinguish ecological function from harmful bloom risk.
7. **Matrixgame Earth Simulator:** define a minimal model, variables, units, tests and uncertainty before adding AI agents.

## 12. Earth Simulator — minimal staged roadmap

Do not begin with a giant planet-wide simulation. Build testable layers:

**Stage A — Local water bucket**
- precipitation, infiltration, soil storage, evapotranspiration, runoff;
- unit tests for mass balance;
- synthetic and sourced input data clearly separated.

**Stage B — Surface energy and temperature**
- simple radiation/energy-balance terms;
- shade and surface properties;
- explicit time step and boundary conditions.

**Stage C — Vegetation and soil**
- simple vegetation cover and soil-moisture response;
- seasonal dynamics and parameter sensitivity;
- no unsupported claim that more vegetation always improves local climate.

**Stage D — Biodiversity and habitat**
- habitat-specific suitability indicators;
- species interactions only where supported by data;
- distinguish habitat suitability from actual population forecasts.

**Stage E — Multi-agent research**
- agents propose hypotheses and tests;
- critic and verifier challenge claims;
- human review before real-world interventions.

**Stage F — Visualization and gamification**
- map layers, time series, causal links and quest progress;
- reward reproducibility, corrections and learning;
- keep rendering separate from simulation calculations.

## 13. Evaluation metrics

Use a dashboard of multiple indicators; do not collapse ecological health into one unvalidated score.

- water balance residual;
- soil moisture and infiltration;
- land-surface temperature with measurement context;
- habitat area and connectivity;
- selected biodiversity indicators with sampling method;
- forest condition indicators;
- energy use and lifecycle emissions;
- model prediction error on held-out data;
- uncertainty and data coverage;
- community participation and accessibility;
- safety incidents and unintended effects.

A project may improve one metric while worsening another. Show trade-offs explicitly.

## 14. Governance and safety

- No ecological intervention without permissions and appropriate expertise.
- Protect sensitive species locations and personal data.
- Respect land rights and local communities.
- Do not introduce non-native species or release engineered organisms.
- Do not culture potentially harmful cyanobacteria outside appropriate supervised facilities.
- Make model limitations, uncertainty, and failed trials visible.
- Require human review for consequential real-world decisions.
- Prefer reversible, low-risk pilots with clear stop conditions.

## 15. Definition of success

The project succeeds when it produces useful, independently reviewable knowledge and practical improvements without shifting harm elsewhere.

The ambition is not to maximize greenery, energy, biomass, or a single score. It is to help people and ecosystems become more resilient, diverse, healthy, and capable of thriving together.

**Make the planet more habitable. Make civilization wiser. Make the future worth living.**

**Let's play and let's be.**
