# Gravity Lab 3D – orbital analogy and model boundaries

## FACT: quantum orbital structure

For the n=2 shell, the p subshell has three orbitals with mutually perpendicular orientations, commonly labeled p_x, p_y and p_z. Each orbital can contain at most two electrons with opposite spin projections. The p orbitals are quantum-mechanical wavefunctions/probability distributions with two lobes and a nodal plane; they are not circular particle trajectories. See OpenStax, *Chemistry*, section 6.3: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry/pages/6-3-development-of-quantum-theory

A complete second electron shell contains 2s² and 2p⁶. Neon has the ground-state configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Therefore the Gravity Lab's current two inner plus six outer bodies do not represent a complete neon atom: the 2s² subshell is absent. The three pairs are only a geometry analogy for three p-orbital orientations; they are not electron probability clouds or electron-spin pairs.

## MODEL: 3D Newtonian dynamics

Each body has position (x,y,z), velocity (vx,vy,vz) and positive mass. Every pair receives a softened Newtonian interaction. The baseline uses a Velocity-Verlet integrator.

The baseline 2+6 layout consists of:
- one central mass;
- two inner masses on an XY-plane diameter;
- two outer masses in each of the XY, XZ and YZ planes, at opposite ends of a circle.

The three outer planes share a center and are mutually perpendicular. All visible trajectories are generated by integrating the current equations of motion; the circles are only reference guides.

## MODEL: optional satellite-only repulsion

The slider parameter lambda modifies satellite–satellite interactions only. For those pairs, the effective coefficient becomes G(1-lambda). The central body remains gravitationally attractive to every satellite.

- lambda = 0: Newtonian gravity only;
- 0 < lambda < 1: reduced mutual satellite attraction;
- lambda = 1: satellite–satellite term cancels in this toy model;
- lambda > 1: net satellite–satellite repulsion.

This is a deliberately simplified, same-sign repulsion experiment. It is not an implementation of electromagnetism or Coulomb's law: there are no charge signs, no unlike-charge attraction, no electron spin and no quantum wavefunctions. Do not interpret the result as a prediction for atom stability or noble-gas chemistry.

## MODEL: geometric symmetry score

The UI reports a custom score between 0 and 100. For each canonical opposite pair in XY, XZ and YZ it calculates:
- radial balance: min(r1,r2) / max(r1,r2);
- antipodal alignment: the negative normalized dot product, clamped to [0,1];
- the mean over the three canonical pairs, multiplied by an occupancy factor exp(-abs(N_outer-6)/6).

In compact form:

```text
S_sym = 100 * mean(radial_balance * antipodal_alignment) * exp(-abs(N_outer - 6) / 6)
```

For the exact initialized layouts the UI reports:
| Outer bodies | Geometric score |
|---:|---:|
| 5 | 56.4% |
| 6 | 100.0% |
| 7 | 84.6% |
| 8 | 71.7% |

This is a hand-designed geometry score, not a quantum number, energy, chemical stability measure, or empirical property of atoms.

## Exploratory perturbation test

A local controlled test offset one outer satellite by +0.5 in x and +0.2 in vy, then ran 2,000 integration steps at repulsion-slider values from 0 to 2. The radial standard deviation was about 0.196 simulation units and maximum radial deviation about 0.55 units across that small sweep. The relative energy drift remained about 0.000041–0.000042%. The slider changed the interaction law, but at these mass ratios and this short run the central mass dominated the overall orbital scale.

These are results for one arbitrary perturbation and chosen numerical parameters. They do not establish an atomic-shell force law.

## Recommended next experiment series

Run a controlled parameter grid rather than immediately tuning toward a desired "noble-gas" outcome:
1. satellite counts 5, 6, 7 and 8;
2. repulsion lambda = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2;
3. exact symmetric starts plus small reproducible position/velocity perturbations;
4. compare radius spread, plane symmetry, pair distances, center-of-mass drift, energy drift and escape/collision events.

A symmetry score can describe this classical toy model. It must remain a model metric, not a substitute for quantum-chemical stability or a measured property of real atoms.
